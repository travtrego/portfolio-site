My Story

The tools changed.
The standard never did.

My career did not arrive in a straight line. It was built across military operations, technical work, public service, accounting, and emerging technology. Every chapter asked something different of me. Together, they taught me what kind of work is worth doing.

01 / Discipline

First, I learned to trust the work.

Six years in United States Navy electronic warfare and technical operations made precision more than a professional preference. In demanding environments, systems awareness, information security, calm analysis, and clear coordination were part of doing the job correctly. There was no value in looking prepared. The work had to withstand pressure.

02 / Range

Then, the field widened.

Civilian experience at Hexagon and U.S. Electrical Services, Inc. (USESI) moved that discipline through technical and operational settings. The environments changed, but the useful habits did not: understand the system, respect the details, communicate clearly, and keep moving when the answer is not obvious.

03 / Advance

Accounting became a new operating language.

Earning a Master of Science in Accounting from the University of Rhode Island in 2024 opened a new arena for the same analytical instincts. At EY, I entered asset-management tax as an associate and advanced to Senior Tax Analyst / Senior Tax Associate. The work added partnership structures, technical research, review, documentation, and deadline-driven judgment to a foundation already built on responsibility.

04 / Build

Eventually, learning was not enough. I had to build.

Analytics, automation, AI-enabled systems, and sustainable finance became more than adjacent interests. They became a way to connect domain knowledge with better decisions. Through Walrus Productions and independent project work, I explore what happens when technical curiosity is held to a professional standard.

Technology should not be theater. A useful system should preserve its history, expose the logic behind its decisions, acknowledge its limits, and leave consequential judgment with people. That principle connects the operator, the accountant, and the builder.

I did not leave one life behind to begin another. I carried every hard-earned discipline forward—and turned a collection of chapters into one direction.

The mission now is simple: understand deeply, build honestly, and make the next system better than the last.
